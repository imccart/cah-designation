\title{
\vspace{-1.5cm}\Huge
Survival versus Scale: The Effects of Critical Access Hospital Designation
}


\author{
\large Ian M. McCarthy\footnote{\scriptsize Department of Economics, Emory University, and NBER. Email: ian.mccarthy@emory.edu} \hspace{0.2cm} \large Katie Leinenbach\footnote{\scriptsize U.S. Department of Justice. Email: katie.leinenbach@gmail.com} }
\date{June 2026}
\maketitle

\onehalfspace

\begin{abstract}
\noindent The Critical Access Hospital (CAH) program, established under the Balanced Budget Act of 1997, provides cost-based Medicare reimbursement to small rural hospitals in exchange for limits on inpatient capacity and service scope. We estimate the effects of CAH designation on hospital finances, capacity, and organizational outcomes using a synthetic difference-in-differences design that exploits the staggered 1999--2002 rollout of state-level CAH availability. We find that CAH designation raises operating margins by three to four percentage points, with no change in liquidity or capital investment, as cost-based reimbursement sustains revenue while expenses fall with scale. Designation also reduces inpatient scale, with significant declines in total beds, obstetric capacity, and nursing staff. Emergency department visits also decline meaningfully, while outpatient volume is largely unchanged. At the same time, CAH designation reduces hospital closures, with no meaningful effect on mergers. These results point to a ``survival versus scale'' tradeoff across hospitals within a state, wherein designation-based subsidies avert closure for a small number of hospitals while inducing widespread inpatient downsizing among the many more hospitals that convert. Because converting hospitals shed more inpatient capacity than averted closures preserve, the program may reduce aggregate rural inpatient capacity even as it keeps hospitals open.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction} 
\label{sec:introduction}
Access to hospital care is central to maintaining quality of life and economic well-being, particularly in rural and underserved communities \citep{ziedan2022}. Beyond their clinical role, hospitals increasingly serve as anchors of local labor markets and economic activity, with the broader health care sector now the nation's largest area of employment \citep{alexander2023}. The United States has nonetheless experienced a steady rise in hospital closures over recent decades, disproportionately concentrated among small and rural facilities \citep{kaufman2016, medpac2021}. These trends have intensified policy concern that financially fragile hospitals may be unable to sustain operations in low-density markets, motivating a range of federal interventions designed to stabilize hospital finances and preserve access to care.

This paper asks whether designation-based hospital subsidies preserve meaningful access to care, or whether they primarily preserve hospital presence at a smaller operational scale. We focus on the Critical Access Hospital (CAH) program, the largest and longest-running federal designation aimed at stabilizing financially distressed hospitals. Conceptually, CAH designation creates an inherent tradeoff. By increasing reimbursement, it may reduce financial distress and prevent closure, but by conditioning eligibility on strict limits to inpatient volume and service scope, it may also constrain hospitals' capacity. Whether and to what extent CAH designation ``helps'' therefore depends on which of these margins dominates.

The CAH program provides a canonical example of current designation-based support programs. Officially established under the Balanced Budget Act of 1997, the program offers eligible hospitals cost-based reimbursement for Medicare patients, effectively guaranteeing a positive margin on a large share of inpatient revenue. In exchange, hospitals must satisfy ongoing eligibility requirements tied to size, location, and service provision, including limits on inpatient census, length of stay, and scope of services. While cost-based reimbursement may reduce short-run financial risk, these institutional features together shape hospitals' behavioral responses to designation. In practice, the accompanying eligibility constraints may induce operational restructuring that offsets the reimbursement benefit, so that designation need not translate into an improvement in hospitals' overall financial position.

Credibly estimating the effects of CAH designation is complicated by staggered policy adoption and endogenous hospital take-up \citep{dalton2003}. States adopted the CAH program at different points between 1999 and 2002, reflecting discretion in the timing of their Medicare Rural Hospital Flexibility Programs, and conditional on availability, conversion to CAH status is a strategic hospital decision that may correlate with unobserved financial trajectories. We exploit this staggered rollout by comparing hospitals that convert to CAH status in early-adopting states to otherwise similar hospitals located in states where CAH designation was not yet available. Our primary control group therefore consists of ``not-yet-treated'' hospitals in slower-adopting states, rather than contemporaneous non-converters within the same state, whose non-conversion may reflect unobserved hospital-level selection. Our results are nonetheless robust to alternative constructions comparing CAH converters and non-converters without the restriction that non-converters reside in slower-adopting states.

We implement this comparison using a Synthetic Difference-in-Differences (SDID) estimator, which constructs weighted combinations of control hospitals to closely match treated hospitals' pre-designation outcome trajectories. This approach is well suited to our setting, where treatment occurs in a small number of cohorts and conventional difference-in-differences comparisons may be sensitive to residual imbalance or differential trends across states. Alongside our SDID estimates, we also report Callaway and Sant'Anna (CS) estimates \citep{callaway2021}, and in the supplemental appendix, we show that our results are qualitatively robust to the interactive fixed effects estimator of \cite{xu2017}, which is designed to more directly account for differential pre-trends.

Using hospital-level CAH designation data from the Flex Monitoring Team, annual survey data from the American Hospital Association (AHA), and financial data from the Healthcare Cost Report Information System (HCRIS), we estimate the effects of CAH designation on hospital finances, investment, capacity, and organizational outcomes. We find that CAH designation raises hospital operating margins by three to four percentage points, an effect that is stable across a range of specifications, estimators, and control group constructions. Decomposing this result into revenue and expenses shows that net patient revenue per bed and total operating expenses per bed both decline following designation, with expenses falling by more, consistent with cost-based reimbursement sustaining revenue as hospitals downsize to satisfy eligibility requirements. Capital expenditures are imprecisely estimated and net fixed assets per bed change little. Designation also reduces inpatient scale, with declines in total beds, obstetric capacity, and nursing staff, consistent with active resizing to meet program requirements. Outpatient visits are largely unchanged, and emergency department visits decline by roughly 17 percent. These reductions partly reflect mechanical compliance with CAH bed limits, but declines in inpatient days per bed suggest that utilization also contracts, not just nominal capacity. We therefore interpret the observed capacity reductions as a behavioral response to the program's eligibility threshold rather than incidental restructuring. Hospitals begin shedding capacity in anticipation of designation, sacrificing scale they might otherwise retain in order to qualify for cost-based reimbursement. At the state level, CAH adoption reduces hospital closures, with largely null effects on merger activity, and converting hospitals become modestly more likely to join systems. Taken together, the results reveal a tension of the CAH program across hospitals within a state, between the few closures it averts and the reduced inpatient capacity among the many hospitals that convert.

While CAH designation improves hospital survival, the implications for aggregate inpatient capacity are less clear. Reduced closures preserve capacity that would otherwise be lost, but hospitals that convert to CAH status also reduce beds and staffing to comply with eligibility requirements. These two effects fall on largely different hospitals. To gauge the net effect, we combine our estimated impacts on closures and within-hospital capacity in an illustrative accounting exercise. Using our point estimates and a conversion rate consistent with observed CAH adoption during the initial rollout, the implied reduction in beds among converting hospitals outweighs the capacity preserved through averted closures under a wide range of assumptions about the size of the marginal hospital saved from closure. The implied net effect on aggregate inpatient capacity is therefore negative. These calculations are necessarily approximate and sensitive to assumptions about conversion intensity and closure risk, but they underscore that the net capacity effect of designation-based subsidies depends on the rate at which eligible hospitals convert.

Our analysis connects to a large literature on the consequences of rural hospital closures for access and patient outcomes \citep{buchmueller2006, holmes2006, lindrooth2003, hsia2011, gujral2019, chandra2023-nber31789, chatterji2024} and to related work on the determinants of hospital exit, often emphasizing financial distress, payer mix, and local market conditions \citep{sloan2003, harrison2007}. This literature establishes that hospital closures reduce access to care and may worsen patient outcomes, though outcomes may also improve when closures reallocate patients to higher-quality hospitals \citep{battaglia2025}. Far less is known about the effects of the policies and programs meant to forestall such closure. Our contribution to this question is threefold.

First, we contribute to work on Medicare's targeted payment adjustments for low-volume and rural providers, and specifically on the CAH program. \cite{gowrisankaran2018} embed conversion within a structural model of hospital demand, finding that designation reduces admissions by approximately 5\% and that the program raises welfare only if it prevents closure of at least 6.5\% of converting hospitals. \cite{carroll2025} simulates the revenue change associated with a move to cost-based reimbursement and finds that it reduces closure rates by roughly 20\% relative to baseline. Each paper estimates a different object. \cite{gowrisankaran2018} estimate the demand and welfare consequences of conversion, with the converting hospital's characteristics and survival taken as given, and \cite{carroll2025} estimates the effect of an eligibility-based revenue change on closure, which averages over eligible hospitals whether or not they convert. We instead estimate the average effect of designation on the capacity, financial performance, and staffing of the hospitals that convert, and the aggregate effect of state CAH adoption on hospital closures.

Second, we contribute to work evaluating how policy-driven changes in hospital resources affect financial performance. Descriptive evidence suggests that CAH conversion improved operating margins \citep{stensland2003, li2009}, though selection into conversion complicates a causal reading, and Medicaid expansions have been found to improve hospital margins and reduce closure risk \citep{blavin2016, lindrooth2018, blavin2021}. We revisit the descriptive margin finding with a design-based estimate and find that designation raises operating margins by three to four percentage points, ultimately also reducing the number of closures. More broadly, we provide the first design-based estimates of how CAH designation affects financial performance and other organizational outcomes within a single framework, using the staggered 1999--2002 rollout of state CAH availability and a common identification strategy throughout. Examining these effects jointly lets us trace how the reimbursement and capacity channels interact, for example showing that margins improve as cost-based reimbursement sustains revenue while expenses generally fall with the reduction in scale.

Third, the finding that hospitals reduce scale to obtain and retain a regulatory designation connects to the literature on size-dependent regulation and notches. Firms respond to size thresholds that trigger regulation \citep{garicano2016}, taxpayers bunch at notches in tax schedules \citep{kleven2013, kleven2016}, and hospitals respond to thresholds in Medicare's payment rules \citep{eliason2018}. The 25-bed limit is analogous in the hospital setting. We document the mass of hospitals at exactly 25 beds that appears after state adoption and show that the reduction in beds rises with a hospital's distance above the limit.

Our findings are particularly salient given renewed policy interest in designation-based approaches to hospital support. For example, the AHA has recently advocated for a new ``Metropolitan Anchor Hospital'' designation aimed at stabilizing urban safety-net hospitals with high Medicaid exposure \citep{hartzman2022}. While such proposals differ in important ways from the CAH program, they share a common logic in attempting to provide targeted financial relief to hospitals deemed essential to local access. Our results suggest that designation-based subsidies can be effective at preserving hospital survival; however, the subsidy also reaches many hospitals at little risk of closure, and those hospitals reduce inpatient scale to qualify for it. As a result, policymakers considering similar interventions should weigh not only whether hospitals remain open, but also how designation affects the quantity and scope of care those hospitals ultimately provide.

\section{Institutional Background}
\label{sec:background}

The CAH program was introduced under the Balanced Budget Act of 1997 as part of a broader federal response to widespread rural hospital closures in the 1980s and early 1990s. The central goal was to preserve access to essential hospital services in sparsely populated areas by replacing inpatient prospective payments with cost-based reimbursements. Under the current program, eligible hospitals are reimbursed at 101\% of their Medicare-allowable costs, providing a guaranteed margin on Medicare patients and protecting hospitals against the risks inherent in the inpatient prospective payment system. By 2021, more than 1,300 hospitals had obtained CAH status, making it the most widely used federal designation to support rural hospital viability \citep{crs2023}.

Eligibility for CAH designation revolves around three criteria: 1) size; 2) rural location; and 3) proximity to other hospitals. In general, CAH-designated hospitals are small, rural, and relatively isolated; however, specific criteria have not necessarily been enforced rigidly over time. For example, hospitals in some otherwise urban Metropolitan Statistical Areas (MSAs) could seek urban-to-rural reclassification, further expanding CAH eligibility. Such a designation was made primarily on a case-by-case basis, generally governed by the extent to which the hospital was geographically isolated and served a rural population. Official requirements for urban-to-rural reclassification were not adopted until the mid-2000s.\footnote{Eventually, hospitals seeking urban-to-rural reclassification could only be classified as rural provided they were located in a sub-county area with a Rural Urban Commuting Area code of 4 or more, but this criterion was not adopted until 2007.} Similarly, CAH designation currently requires hospitals to be at least 35 miles from the nearest other hospital on ``primary roads'' or 15 miles on ``secondary roads,'' but prior to 2006, states could exempt hospitals from these proximity requirements as part of a ``necessary provider'' exception.\footnote{While the necessary provider exception was no longer allowed after 2006, those with such an exemption were grandfathered in and maintained their CAH status beyond 2006. When combining both rural location and distance criteria, an Office of the Inspector General report in 2013 found that ``Nearly two-thirds of CAHs would not meet the location requirements if required to re-enroll.''} Ultimately, while the CAH program is federally authorized, states have played a key role in determining which hospitals may obtain CAH status via their Medicare Rural Hospital Flexibility Program (the ``Flex Program''), which designates a state agency to review applications and determine CAH eligibility. Hospitals in a state could not be designated until the state had established its Flex program and designated that agency, and most states began certifying hospitals between 1999 and 2002, although a few states had earlier designations from the predecessor Rural Primary Care Hospital program. Historically, eligibility criteria have therefore been more flexible in practice, enabling states to adapt the program to local hospital markets.

Once designated, CAHs must maintain ongoing service and size restrictions, including treating no more than 25 inpatients per day, maintaining an average length of stay below 96 hours, and offering 24-hour emergency care. The tradeoff for receiving cost-based reimbursement is therefore reduced scope and operational flexibility, as hospitals must continuously satisfy these requirements to retain CAH status.

Other Medicare payment designations, such as low-volume hospital (LVH), Medicare dependent hospital (MDH), and sole community hospital (SCH), serve related purposes but have been less consistently available. For example, the MDH and LVH programs expired in FY 2017 before being renewed through 2024. By contrast, the CAH designation has existed continuously since 1997, with precursors from state-level demonstration programs, and now accounts for over half of all such special designations \citep{crs2023}. The stability and breadth of CAH designation make it central to understanding federal policy toward rural hospitals, with implications for urban hospitals in light of recent advocacy for metropolitan anchor hospital designation.

To organize the empirical predictions, consider a hospital providing three services, inpatient care ($I$), emergency care ($E$), and other outpatient care ($O$), to publicly insured (Medicare, $g$) and commercially insured ($c$) patients. Let $q_{sk}$ denote the quantity of service $s \in \{I, E, O\}$ provided to payer $k \in \{g, c\}$ at per-unit price $p_{sk}$, and let $\theta_{it} \in \{0,1\}$ indicate whether hospital $i$ holds CAH status in year $t$. CAH designation affects the hospital through two channels: a higher per-unit Medicare price under cost-based reimbursement, $p_{sg}(1) > p_{sg}(0)$ for every service $s$, and a capacity constraint that limits inpatient volume. Commercial prices do not depend on designation. Total operating costs $C(q_I, q_E, q_O)$ depend on the volume of each service, $q_s = q_{sg} + q_{sc}$, and need not be separable across services. Per-period operating profits are then:
\begin{equation}
\pi = \sum_{s \in \{I,E,O\}} \left[ q_{sg} \cdot p_{sg}(\theta) + q_{sc} \cdot p_{sc} \right] - C(q_I, q_E, q_O), \quad \text{subject to } q_I \leq \bar{Q}(\theta),
\label{eq:cah_profit}
\end{equation}
where $\bar{Q}(1) < \bar{Q}(0)$ reflects the CAH limits on inpatient census and average length of stay. Emergency and other outpatient services are not capped, and cost-based reimbursement applies to Medicare patients in all three services. This framework generates three sets of testable predictions, corresponding to capacity, financial, and organizational outcomes, which we consider in turn.

First, consider capacity. Let $q_s^0$ denote the hospital's volume of service $s$ before designation. The capacity constraint binds only for hospitals with $q_I^0 > \bar{Q}(1)$, which must reduce inpatient volume by $\Delta q_I = \bar{Q}(1) - q_I^0 < 0$, a reduction that grows with the hospital's distance above the limit. For hospitals already at or below the limit the constraint is slack, and the cap implies no reduction. Where the limit is binding, beds and inpatient staffing should fall with inpatient volume. Moreover, since a hospital need only reduce to $\bar{Q}(1)$, post-designation inpatient beds and volumes should bunch at the limit itself. Section~\ref{sec:capacity} and the supplemental appendix test these predictions, namely a reduction in beds that is concentrated at the 25-bed limit, rises with a hospital's distance above it, and is absent for hospitals already at or below it.

Figure~\ref{fig:anticipation} confirms that this constraint binds in practice. Panel~(a) plots mean total beds for hospitals that eventually receive CAH designation (cohorts 1999--2005, hospitals with 50 or fewer beds in at least one year), centered on the year of designation. Mean beds decline from approximately 49 five years before designation to 46 one year before, then drop sharply to 42 at designation and continue falling to 35 five years after. The 25th percentile reaches the 25-bed statutory limit one year before designation and remains at 25 thereafter. Panel~(b) shows a parallel decline in inpatient days per bed, from 159 to 123 over the same window.\footnote{The declines in Figure~\ref{fig:anticipation} further suggest that hospitals begin actively restructuring operations in anticipation of CAH conversion, which we examine in Section~\ref{sec:anticipation}.}

The eligibility limits do not directly apply to the volume of emergency or other outpatient services, so the hospital provides each of these services up to the regular profit maximizing condition, $p_s(\theta) = C_s(q_I, q_E, q_O)$. We can further write the average price the hospital receives for service $s$ as $p_s(\theta) = \omega_s \, p_{sg}(\theta) + (1 - \omega_s) \, p_{sc}$, where $\omega_s$ is the Medicare share of its volume, which we hold fixed. Designation affects both sides of this equality. The payment rises by $\Delta p_s = \omega_s \left[ p_{sg}(1) - p_{sg}(0) \right] \geq 0$, since only Medicare patients are paid at the new rate. The cost of a unit of service $s$ also moves, because that cost depends on inpatient volume, which falls where the cap binds. The hospital then adjusts its volume of service $s$ until cost and payment are equal again. Assuming emergency and other outpatient care are separable from each other in cost, so that each interacts with inpatient care but not with the other, that adjustment is approximately
\begin{equation*}
  \Delta q_s \approx \frac{\Delta p_s - C_{sI} \, \Delta q_I}{C_{ss}},
\end{equation*}
where $C_{ss} > 0$ is the rate at which the cost of a unit rises with volume. The numerator is the gap that opens between payment and cost at the hospital's original volume, and dividing by $C_{ss}$ turns that gap into a change in volume, so a service whose cost climbs steeply responds less. The numerator's first term, $\Delta p_s$, raises volume at every converting hospital. Its second term is present only where the cap binds, since it scales with $\Delta q_I$, and its sign depends on how the service enters costs alongside inpatient care. An uncapped service contracts along with inpatient care when the two are cost complements ($C_{sI} < 0$), as when they share staff and fixed inputs, and expands when they are cost substitutes ($C_{sI} > 0$), as when they compete for the same inputs. Emergency care draws on the same acute-care staff as the inpatient unit and is the entry point for admissions, so we expect the cost term to outweigh the price term, giving $\Delta q_E < 0$. Other outpatient care is closer to separable from inpatient care, so we expect $\Delta q_O \geq 0$. Section~\ref{sec:capacity} tests these predictions as well, namely a decline in emergency visits and no decline in outpatient visits.

Second, the net effect on financial performance is ambiguous. Consider the operating margin, $m = 1 - C/R$, where $R = \sum_{s} \left[ q_{sg} \cdot p_{sg}(\theta) + q_{sc} \cdot p_{sc} \right]$ denotes total revenue. Designation moves the margin by
\begin{equation*}
\Delta m \approx (1 - m) \left( \frac{\Delta R}{R} - \frac{\Delta C}{C} \right),
\end{equation*}
so the margin rises when revenue falls by proportionally less than costs. Designation raises revenue through the higher Medicare rate on the volume the hospital already has, and lowers it through the reduction in inpatient volume, with the uncapped services moving as described above. Costs fall with those same volumes, but the higher rate does not raise them. If hospitals downsize proportionally, reducing beds, staff, and services together, revenue and costs fall at similar rates and the price increase raises the margin. If instead fixed costs do not shrink with volume, costs fall by proportionally less than revenue and the margin can fall despite the higher Medicare rate. The sign of the margin effect therefore depends on the relative magnitude of the per-unit reimbursement gain and the gap between the volume-driven contractions in revenue and costs. Section~\ref{sec:financial} finds that revenue and expenses fall together with scale, with expenses falling slightly more than revenue, and that the margin rises.

Third, CAH designation may affect organizational outcomes. A hospital exits when $\pi$ falls below a threshold $\underline{\pi}$, reflecting the opportunity cost of continued operation, so a higher margin lowers closure risk directly. Even if designation does not raise expected profits, cost-based reimbursement replaces variable prospective payments with a guaranteed cost-plus rate, reducing the variance of Medicare revenue and lowering the probability that $\pi < \underline{\pi}$ in any given period. This variance-reduction channel can decrease closure risk without improving average profitability. Mergers and system membership represent alternative organizational responses to financial pressure, with the incentive to affiliate depending on both the level and variability of standalone profits, although designation may also make a hospital a more attractive affiliate for a system that would share in its cost-based reimbursement. Section~\ref{sec:orgchange} finds fewer closures in adopting states, no systematic change in mergers, and a modest rise in system membership. These mechanisms guide our empirical analysis in Section~\ref{sec:design}.

\section{Data Sources and Key Variables}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Data Sources}

\paragraph{\textit{American Hospital Association (AHA) Annual Survey.}}  
The AHA survey provides a rich set of hospital characteristics for essentially all non-federal general acute care hospitals from 1980 onward, including information on ownership, size, teaching status, system membership, CAH designation, and detailed location information. The AHA surveys also include annual summaries of organizational changes in the ``Summary of Changes'' files, including hospital closures and mergers.

\paragraph{\textit{Flex Monitoring Team CAH Supplement.}}  
To track CAH status, we supplement the AHA surveys with data from the Flex Monitoring Team, which is ``a consortium of researchers from the Universities of Minnesota, North Carolina at Chapel Hill, and Southern Maine'' tasked with evaluating the Medicare Rural Hospital Flexibility Program. These data provide effective CAH designation dates, hospital locations, and capacity. Because the AHA’s critical access indicator is incomplete, we fuzzy-match the supplement to the AHA survey to recover accurate designation histories. This linkage allows us to observe the staggered rollout of CAH status beginning in the mid-1990s.\footnote{The official CAH program launched as part of the Balanced Budget Act of 1997; however, the CAH program was modeled after pre-existing demonstration programs such as the Rural Primary Care Hospital designation. The Flex Monitoring Team therefore identifies a handful of hospitals as CAHs dating back to 1994.}

\paragraph{\textit{Healthcare Cost Report Information System (HCRIS).}}  
Medicare cost reports provide annual financial statements for virtually all facilities that participate in Medicare, and they are the source of all of our financial measures. Because a hospital receives a new Medicare provider number upon CAH designation, we link each converting hospital to the cost reports filed under its earlier provider number by matching on state, zip code, city, and name, so that its financial series is measured from the same source before and after designation. The supplemental appendix describes this crosswalk.\footnote{We also link IRS Form 990 filings for nonprofit hospitals to the AHA survey using Employer Identification Numbers (EINs) and fuzzy matching of names and locations. Because filings are made by the tax-exempt entity rather than the hospital and define revenue and expenses more broadly than the cost report, we do not combine them with HCRIS, and none of our financial measures use them.}


\subsection{Key Variable Construction}

\paragraph{\textit{CAH designation.}}  
Our treatment variable is an indicator for whether a hospital is designated as a CAH in a given year. We construct this using both the AHA and the Flex Monitoring Team supplement, with the latter resolving discrepancies and providing precise effective dates. At the state level, we define treatment timing as the year of first designation in that state. Our construction of treated and control hospitals and states is presented in more detail in Section~\ref{sec:design}.

\paragraph{\textit{Financial performance.}}
We construct six hospital-level financial outcomes using the HCRIS data.\footnote{Details of the construction of our financial measures are presented in the supplemental appendix.} First, we measure basic hospital profitability using the operating margin, defined as operating income divided by net patient revenue. This captures internal resources available to support ongoing operations. Second, we proxy short-run financial flexibility using the current ratio, defined as current assets divided by current liabilities, so that higher values indicate a greater ability to meet near-term obligations without external financing. Third, to decompose changes in operating margin into their component parts, we separately measure net patient revenue per bed and total operating expenses per bed, each deflated to constant 2010 dollars using the Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers (CPI-U) and normalized by pre-period mean bed count. These component measures allow us to assess whether changes in margins reflect movements in revenue, costs, or both. We winsorize values at the 5th and 95th percentiles within each year, separately for eventual CAHs and other hospitals and linearly interpolate any within-hospital gap years.

\paragraph{\textit{Capital stock and investment.}}
We measure capital intensity as real net fixed assets per bed, where net fixed assets include land, buildings, and equipment net of accumulated depreciation. We deflate this measure using the CPI-U and normalize by the hospital's pre-period bed count. We further construct real capital expenditure per bed as a measure of the intensity of investment in long-term assets relative to hospital size. We approximate this using the year-to-year change in gross fixed assets (net plant plus accumulated depreciation) from HCRIS. We again winsorize values at the 5th and 95th percentiles within each year, separately for eventual CAHs and other hospitals and linearly interpolate any within-hospital gap years.

\paragraph{\textit{Capacity and staffing.}}
We measure hospital capacity and staffing using three complementary indicators drawn from the AHA Annual Survey: total hospital beds, obstetric (OB) beds, and full-time equivalent (FTE) registered nurses. Total beds capture overall inpatient capacity, while OB beds provide a targeted measure of capacity in maternity services, a clinically and financially salient service line for many small and rural hospitals. FTE registered nurses proxy for labor inputs and operational capacity, reflecting hospitals' ability to staff inpatient services and sustain day-to-day clinical operations. We note that the AHA measure of total beds (BDTOT) reflects total staffed beds, which may not correspond precisely to the CAH statutory bed limit that applies to acute care inpatient beds and excludes swing beds and certain other categories. This distinction does not affect our qualitative findings but means that our measure of total beds may modestly overstate the relevant bed count for some hospitals. As with the financial and capital measures, we winsorize these values at the 5th and 95th percentiles within each year, separately for eventual CAHs and other hospitals and linearly interpolate any within-hospital gap years.

\paragraph{\textit{Organizational change.}}  
System membership is measured at the hospital level based on the AHA Annual Survey. Closures and mergers are identified using the AHA’s summary of changes. We construct indicators for closure and merger events at the hospital-year level and aggregate to counts at the state-year level. To facilitate comparisons across states and over time, we rescale our estimates by the mean pre-period hospital count in treated states, reporting results as counts per 100 hospitals. We discuss our state-level analysis of mergers and closures in more detail in Section~\ref{sec:orgchange}. 

\paragraph{\textit{Other variables.}}  
As discussed previously, bed size, rural status, and proximity to other hospitals are strongly correlated with CAH status, even though Flex programs did not always enforce these criteria rigidly. We use bed size to restrict the sample to hospitals for which conversion was plausibly feasible, requiring at least one year with a bed count of 50 or fewer, although results are not sensitive to reasonable alternative threshold values. To measure geographic isolation, we compute the distance from each hospital to its nearest neighbor, combining ZIP-code--based and latitude--longitude measures and taking the minimum of the two to approximate travel distance. We use distance in addition to hospital ownership and pre-treatment financial health as dimensions of heterogeneity in Section~\ref{sec:heterogeneity}. Consistent with standard practice for SDID, we do not include these variables as regression covariates, as the estimator addresses confounding through optimal unit and time weighting.

\subsection{Descriptive Statistics}
\label{sec:desc}

Our hospital-level dataset covers 6,911 unique hospitals observed at any point from 1980 to 2019. We restrict to general, acute care, community hospitals, and we exclude Maryland, Alaska, Hawaii, and all territories (Puerto Rico, Guam, and others).\footnote{Maryland is excluded because it has its own state version of critical access hospital designation \citep{mhcc2020}.} The resulting panel contains consistent hospital identifiers, CAH designation histories, and other variables discussed previously.

Figure~\ref{fig:desc-panelview-treat} visualizes the staggered rollout of CAH designation at the state level. The white tiles in Figure~\ref{fig:desc-panelview-treat} denote years in which no hospital in that state had received CAH designation, and the gray tiles reflect years in which at least one hospital in that state had received CAH designation. From Figure~\ref{fig:desc-panelview-treat}, we see CAH designations first arise as part of the early demonstration programs in 1994 and 1995, with 5 states having at least one hospital with such a designation by 1995. The program rapidly expanded once officially launched in 1999, with 19 states having at least one CAH in 1999, another 8 states in 2000, another 8 states in 2001, and 2 additional states in 2002. Figure~\ref{fig:desc-hosp-types} presents the analogous hospital-level data, reflecting the rise of CAH designation between 1999 and 2005, with the count of CAH-designated hospitals stabilizing around 1,250 hospitals by 2005. Figure~\ref{fig:desc-hosp-types} is also consistent with a more malleable CAH designation policy prior to 2006, with very few new designations after 2006.

We limit our empirical analysis to the years from 1995 through 2010 in order to align with the core years over which states adopted CAH designations (1999 through 2002) and avoid increasingly noisy estimates of operating margins in prior years. With this additional restriction to 1995--2010, Table~\ref{tab:summary-by-cah} provides descriptive statistics for CAHs and non-CAHs, focusing on the key CAH eligibility criteria of bed size, rural location, and proximity to other hospitals, as well as our primary outcome variables. For CAHs, we present summary statistics separately for the years prior to CAH designation, the years with CAH designation, and all years combined (columns 1--3 of Table~\ref{tab:summary-by-cah}).\footnote{Hospitals that received CAH designation after 2010 (before 1995) therefore enter into the ``pre'' (``post'') values but not vice versa, hence the different counts of pre and post within CAH-designated hospitals.} 

We highlight three points from Table~\ref{tab:summary-by-cah}. First, CAHs are significantly smaller, more rural, and less proximate to other hospitals than are non-CAHs, consistent with the underlying CAH eligibility criteria. Second, again demonstrating the relative flexibility in CAH eligibility criteria, there remains some overlap in the distribution of these characteristics between CAHs and non-CAHs. For example, among hospitals receiving CAH designation, the 90th percentile in bed size in pre-CAH years was 107. Meanwhile, the 10th percentile of bed size for non-CAHs was 49. CAH designation therefore does not rigidly imply that hospitals are rural, smaller, or less proximate to others than non-CAHs, although on average this remains true. Third, we see that hospitals already with CAH designation are consistently smaller in terms of bed size than similar hospitals that eventually also receive CAH designation. For example, after receiving CAH designation, the mean CAH bed size and mean inpatient days per bed were 41 and 136, respectively. Meanwhile, the mean bed size and inpatient days per bed for CAHs before receiving designation were 55 and 167. Consistent with Figure~\ref{fig:anticipation}, the level changes in bed sizes and inpatient days per bed reflect the practical constraints placed on hospitals once they receive CAH designation, wherein such hospitals appear to reduce their number of beds and inpatient days in order to receive and maintain CAH designation.

\section{Effects of CAH Designation}
\label{sec:design}

Guided by our discussion in Section~\ref{sec:background} and surrounding Eq.~\eqref{eq:cah_profit}, we pursue a difference-in-differences identification strategy exploiting differential timing of CAH status and statewide CAH availability. Our preferred estimation strategy employs the SDID estimator to estimate the causal effect of CAH designation \citep{arkhangelsky2021}. We focus first on the effects of CAH on hospital finances, capacity, and investment, then turn to organizational outcomes including closures, mergers, and system membership.

Our goal is to estimate the effect of the CAH program on the hospitals receiving the designation. Because the program combines cost-based reimbursement with eligibility rules that a hospital must meet to obtain and keep the designation, the restructuring hospitals undertake to meet those rules is part of the effect we estimate. We date treatment at the year of designation, when cost-based reimbursement begins, and examine the restructuring that precedes it in Section~\ref{sec:anticipation}. In this design, treated hospitals are those that convert, and our preferred controls are comparable hospitals in states where the program was not yet available. The exceptions are closures and mergers, which are rare and have within-state spillovers, so we estimate the effect of state CAH adoption on closure and merger counts at the state level (Section~\ref{sec:orgchange}).

\subsection{Empirical Strategy}
\label{sec:design-methods}

SDID estimates nonnegative unit weights on the comparison pool to reproduce the treated cohort’s pre-period trajectory and time weights to align pre-period averages, yielding a post-period average treatment effect on the treated (ATT) and treated and synthetic paths. We report cohort-level paths to make the identifying variation transparent and then aggregate across cohorts in event time by weighting each cohort’s path by its treated-unit count. We present jackknife standard errors, with permutation inference summarized in the supplemental appendix.

Formally, let $Y_{it}$ denote the outcome for unit $i$ in period $t$, $D_{it}$ an indicator for treatment, and $G$ the set of treated units in a given cohort $c$ with pre-period $\mathcal{T}_0$ and post-period $\mathcal{T}_1$. The SDID estimator chooses unit weights $\omega=(\omega_j)_{j\in C}$ for comparison units $C$ and time weights $\lambda=(\lambda_t)_{t\in\mathcal{T}_0}$ to minimize
\begin{equation*}
\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_0}\Big( \bar Y_{Gt} - \sum_{j\in C}\omega_j Y_{jt}\Big)^2 
\quad \text{and} \quad
\bar Y_{G\mathcal{T}_0} - \sum_{t\in \mathcal{T}_0}\lambda_t\sum_{j\in C}\omega_j Y_{jt},
\end{equation*}
subject to $\omega_j\ge 0$, $\sum_{j\in C}\omega_j=1$, and $\lambda_t\ge 0$, $\sum_{t\in \mathcal{T}_0}\lambda_t=1$. The resulting synthetic controls reproduce the treated cohort’s pre-period trajectory and average level. The cohort-specific ATT is then
\begin{equation*}
\widehat{\tau}_c = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_1|}\sum_{t\in \mathcal{T}_1}\Big(\bar Y_{Gt} - \sum_{j\in C}\omega_j Y_{jt}\Big).
\end{equation*}
We report both the path $\bar Y_{Gt}-\sum_{j\in C}\omega_j Y_{jt}$ over $t$ and the pooled ATT $\widehat\tau_c$. Cohort estimates are then aggregated by weighting each cohort’s path by its treated-unit count. We summarize effects across cohorts using treated-unit--count weights for the pooled ATT and compute pooled standard errors using the same weighting scheme, treating cohort-specific estimates as approximately independent.\footnote{Independence here is only to facilitate aggregation across treated cohorts, rather than an assumption per se. We also present cohort-specific effects and confidence intervals that need no such claim of independence across cohorts.}

We prefer an SDID approach for two reasons. First, our analysis relies on a small number of states and hospitals receiving CAH designation, along with a limited pool of comparable control hospitals, for which SDID methods may offer improved performance in finite samples. Second, the criteria for CAH designation are not strictly mechanical in practice, which will tend to introduce unobserved heterogeneity and potential endogeneity into the treatment selection process. Hospitals that receive CAH designation may therefore systematically differ in a time-varying way from a simply defined control group (e.g., small, rural hospitals in the same state). In assigning optimal weights to minimize the pre-treatment outcome differences, SDID methods intuitively create a control group for which the parallel trends assumption is more likely to be satisfied. Nonetheless, we also report CS estimates alongside our SDID estimates.\footnote{We use a not-yet-treated comparison group and include no covariates, and we aggregate dynamic effects over the same five-year post-designation window as the SDID estimates. Unlike SDID, the CS estimator allows for unbalanced panels, so its counts of treated and control units differ from those reported here.} In the supplemental appendix, we further estimate an interactive fixed effects (IFE) estimator under a latent factor structure \citep{xu2017}. The appendix also reports all results based on our alternative eligibility-restricted design, complete CS event studies for all outcomes, and a suite of SDID-specific diagnostics, including comparisons of balanced-panel sample sizes relative to the full estimation sample, formal pre-trend tests, and measures of weight concentration across control units.

Identifying the effect of designation from this comparison requires that the timing of state adoption be unrelated to trends in hospital outcomes. To assess the plausibility of that requirement, we relate each state's adoption year to its hospital market structure, closure rates, operating margins, and unemployment over 1990 to 1996, with full results in the supplemental appendix. We find that earlier-adopting states are more rural and have more small hospitals (37 percent of hospitals are rural in the 1999 cohort against 17 percent in 2001), and their pre-period margins are about 2.5 percentage points lower; however, after accounting for rurality and the number of small hospitals, neither closure rates nor unemployment predicts when a state adopted the program. This pattern is consistent with the program reaching its target population first rather than with states responding to hospital distress. This analysis suggests that, to the extent states differ by adoption cohort, those differences are mainly in levels rather than trends. Because SDID reweights control hospitals to match the treated cohort's pre-designation path, level differences of this kind do not threaten the estimates.

The remaining concern is a difference in trends. Figures~\ref{fig:pretrend-financial}--\ref{fig:pretrend-org} plot the treated and control means before and after designation for each outcome, and they show converters and controls moving in parallel until converters begin restructuring in the year or two before designation. That restructuring is part of the program's effect rather than a pre-existing trend, and we examine it directly in Section~\ref{sec:anticipation}.

In the remainder of this section, we discuss results by outcome category, beginning with financial performance and proceeding through investment, capacity, and organizational outcomes. Table~\ref{tab:sdid-overall} summarizes the pooled SDID and CS estimates for the state-timing design. Throughout, we also report the corresponding estimates from the eligibility-restricted design and from the IFE estimator, both of which appear in full in the supplemental appendix.

\subsection{Financial Performance}
\label{sec:financial}

We begin by examining how CAH designation affects hospital operating margins, the outcome most directly linked to the reimbursement change in Section~\ref{sec:background}. In this analysis, the unit of observation is a hospital-year, treatment is defined at the hospital level, and a hospital is considered treated once it receives CAH designation. The sample is restricted to facilities plausibly eligible for designation (i.e., hospitals with 50 or fewer beds in at least one year), excluding hospitals with missing financial data. This setup isolates the direct effect of adopting CAH status on hospital margins.

For each cohort, we construct a balanced panel of treated units and comparison hospitals over a symmetric window around the adoption year. For each treated cohort $i$, defined by the year of first CAH designation, we construct the treated group as hospitals that receive their initial CAH designation in year $i$ and are observed over the event window $[i-\text{pre}, i+\text{post}]$. We use five years before and after designation for the capacity and organizational outcomes. For the financial outcomes, whose series begin in 1996, we use three pre-designation years so that each cohort has a full pre-period. The comparison pool combines two groups: (i) ``never-treated'' hospitals located in states that never adopt CAH designation during our sample, and (ii) ``not-yet-treated'' hospitals in later-adopting states that have no CAH hospitals as of the cohort year and do not convert within the event window. This design means that, for a 1999 CAH in Arkansas, for example, we can use non-CAH Kentucky hospitals through the event window as controls (Kentucky’s first CAH arrives in 2000), but we do not include as controls any hospitals in Georgia, where CAH was already available in 1999. The restriction on a state's first CAH designation is intended to reduce reliance on comparisons between early adopters and same-state or same-cohort non-adopters whose decision not to convert may reflect unobserved, time-varying hospital characteristics (e.g., management quality, lobbying success, or local politics) that also directly affect the outcomes we study. This restriction therefore pushes the design toward comparing treated hospitals to similar hospitals in ``slower'' Flex states that had not yet opened the CAH option in the cohort year, while still allowing those states to roll out CAH later and using their not-yet-treated hospitals as controls.\footnote{In the supplemental appendix, we show that our results are robust to varying the required lag in state-level CAH adoption, shortening the post-period event window (which relaxes the requirement that control hospitals remain unconverted for the full baseline window, allowing hospitals that converted soon after their state opened the CAH program to serve as controls), excluding hospitals that converted immediately upon state implementation, and excluding never-treated states from the control pool. The resulting estimates are less precise in some cases but broadly consistent with our main specification. Our results are also consistent with alternative sample constructions based solely on CAH versus non-CAH status at the hospital level, without imposing any requirements on state-level availability.}

Figure~\ref{fig:sdid-financial} reports SDID estimates of CAH designation on operating margin, the current ratio, and the components of operating margin (i.e., net patient revenue per bed and total operating expenses per bed). The pooled SDID ATT for operating margin is $0.033$ (95\% CI $[0.01, 0.05]$), an increase of 3.3 percentage points from a pre-designation mean of $-0.12$ among converting hospitals in the estimation sample. The CS estimate is similar at $0.041$ (95\% CI $[0.03, 0.05]$). As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:background}, the net effect on margins reflects the interaction of higher Medicare reimbursement with the operational restructuring induced by eligibility constraints. Decomposing the margin into its components clarifies this mechanism.

Panels (c) and (d) of Figure~\ref{fig:sdid-financial} show that both net patient revenue per bed and total operating expenses per bed decline following CAH designation, with similar magnitudes. The SDID estimates imply reductions of roughly \$19,500 in net patient revenue and \$21,600 in operating expenses per bed (in 2010 dollars), or about 8 and 9 percent of their pre-designation means. Because both are normalized by the pre-designation bed count, these are declines in total revenue and total expenses, and both are of similar size to the decline in beds (Section~\ref{sec:capacity}). Revenue and expenses therefore fall together with scale, with expenses falling slightly more than revenue, consistent with cost-based reimbursement sustaining revenue as the hospital sheds inpatient capacity.\footnote{In more formal tests of differential pre-trends reported in the supplemental appendix, operating margin shows statistically significant differential pre-trends in the 2000 and 2001 cohorts and total operating expenses per bed in the 1999 cohort, while net patient revenue per bed shows none. These pre-trends are consistent with the pre-designation restructuring documented in Section~\ref{sec:anticipation}, in which hospitals begin to shed scale, and margins dip, in the year before designation. Pre-period sensitivity analysis in the supplemental appendix shows that SDID point estimates are stable across pre-period lengths of 2--5 years.}

The estimated effect on operating margins is positive across a range of specifications, estimators, and control group constructions. Point estimates range from 0.033 to 0.040 as we vary the pre-period length from 2 to 5 years, and remain between 0.020 and 0.041 as we vary the required lag in state-level CAH adoption, relax state-timing restrictions in the eligibility-restricted sample, or raise the bed-size cutoff to 75 beds. A 25-bed cutoff gives a larger but imprecise estimate (0.115, 95\% CI $[-0.01, 0.23]$), and the IFE estimate is 0.026, both reported in the supplemental appendix. A permutation test with 500 placebo reassignments of treatment gives a $p$-value of 0.006.

For the current ratio, the SDID pooled ATT is $-0.239$ (95\% CI $[-0.54, 0.06]$) and the CS estimate is $0.116$ (95\% CI $[-0.04, 0.27]$). The two differ in sign, amounting to a decline of about 9 percent of the pre-designation ratio and an increase of about 4 percent, although neither is statistically significant. The IFE estimator yields an estimated effect of $-0.094$ (95\% CI $[-0.22, 0.03]$). Taken together, we find no evidence that designation meaningfully affects short-run liquidity, though these estimates are generally imprecise and we cannot rule out relatively large effects in either direction.

\subsection{Capital Stock and Investment}
\label{sec:capital}
Figure~\ref{fig:sdid-capital} examines whether CAH designation translates into changes in capital intensity (net fixed assets per bed) and investment flows (capital expenditures per bed). The estimated effect on the installed capital stock is statistically indistinguishable from zero, with a pooled ATT for net fixed assets per bed of $0.004$ (95\% CI $[-0.02, 0.03]$). Estimated effects on capital expenditures are positive but imprecise (ATT $= 0.976$, 95\% CI $[-0.38, 2.33]$), reflecting the inherently lumpy nature of capital investment in small hospitals. Taken together, the financial results describe a hospital that becomes smaller in scale and more profitable, with no change in liquidity or capital intensity over the time horizon we study. This is consistent with cost-based reimbursement sustaining revenue while expenses fall with the reduction in scale.

\subsection{Capacity and Staffing}
\label{sec:capacity}
Figure~\ref{fig:sdid-capacity} shows that CAH designation is associated with a contraction in both capacity and labor inputs. Total staffed beds decline following adoption, with a pooled ATT of $-4.081$ beds (95\% CI $[-7.70, -0.46]$), roughly 11 percent of the pre-designation mean of 36 beds among converting hospitals in the estimation sample. The reduction is particularly salient in obstetric capacity, with OB beds falling by 0.468 (95\% CI $[-0.81, -0.12]$). Staffing moves in the same direction, with FTE registered nurses falling by 2.023 (95\% CI $[-3.44, -0.61]$). These patterns are consistent with the program's mechanical constraints (e.g., inpatient census limits and related operating requirements) and with hospitals actively resizing service capacity to obtain and maintain CAH status. Importantly, the staffing response tracks the scale response in beds, suggesting that CAH designation primarily induces a reduction in operational scale rather than a pure substitution from capital to labor or vice versa. The cohort-specific estimates reported in Figure~\ref{fig:sdid-capacity} show the bed reduction growing across cohorts, from $-0.3$ beds for the 1999 cohort to $-3.0$ for 2000 and $-6.5$ for 2001, with similar OB bed and staffing effects in the 2000 and 2001 cohorts. Inpatient days per bed also decline following designation (ATT $= -6.7$, 95\% CI $[-18.5, 5.2]$), and the decline is larger and precisely estimated in the eligibility-restricted design ($-17.6$, 95\% CI $[-21.2, -14.0]$), consistent with the capacity reduction reflecting genuine contraction in utilization rather than the shedding of already-empty beds. Some converting hospitals had low pre-conversion occupancy and could meet CAH bed limits with minimal disruption to care provision, but the joint decline in beds, staffing, and utilization indicates that designation induced real operational downsizing across the sample. The CS estimates in Table~\ref{tab:sdid-overall} match in sign, are close in magnitude for total beds and OB beds, and are larger for FTE nurses and inpatient days per bed.

The bed reduction is concentrated at the 25-bed limit. Figure~\ref{fig:bed-notch} plots the distribution of beds in adopting states before and after adoption. The share of hospitals at exactly 25 beds rises from 1.8 percent to 16.6 percent, with the mass just above the limit moving onto it. States that had not yet adopted show no such mass in the same years (1.0 to 1.8 percent of hospital-years at 25 beds), and within adopting states the mass comes almost entirely from eventual converters, 28 percent of which are at exactly 25 beds after adoption against 1 percent of never-converters. As shown in the supplemental appendix, the reduction in beds also rises with a hospital's distance above the limit.

That said, designation does not reduce care across the board. Figure~\ref{fig:sdid-visits} and Table~\ref{tab:visits} report outpatient visits and emergency department visits per pre-designation bed, the same normalization as our revenue and expense outcomes, along with births as annual counts, all from the AHA survey. Outpatient visits per bed are essentially unchanged, with an SDID ATT of 1.21 (95\% CI $[-57.9, 60.3]$), and non-emergency outpatient visits per bed are if anything higher. Emergency department visits per bed fall by 18.08 (95\% CI $[-27.4, -8.7]$), roughly 17 percent of the pre-designation mean of 104 emergency visits per bed among converting hospitals.\footnote{In the CS event studies reported in the supplemental appendix, outpatient visits, emergency department visits, and births all show differential pre-trends, declining relative to the not-yet-treated pool over the five years before designation. These pre-trends reflect differences between converters and the unweighted control pool, which the SDID unit weights minimize.} Births fall by about 10 per year (95\% CI $[-20.8, -0.2]$), in line with the reduction in obstetric beds. The CS estimates match in sign throughout, are close in magnitude for outpatient and emergency visits, and are larger for births. The contraction is therefore concentrated in inpatient, emergency, and obstetric services, with no meaningful change in outpatient volume.

\subsection{Pre-Designation Restructuring}
\label{sec:anticipation}

Because a hospital must meet the census and bed limits to receive CAH designation, it might begin the restructuring process in advance of an official designation. Figure~\ref{fig:anticipation} provides summary evidence of such adjustments, where we see that mean beds among eventual converters fall from 49 five years before designation to 46 one year before, then to 42 at designation and 35 five years after. Most of the reduction in beds therefore occurs at or after designation, but some precedes it. Inpatient days and nurse staffing appear to adjust in the same way in the year before designation (Figure~\ref{fig:pretrend-capacity}).

We would then expect pre-designation changes in financial outcomes to follow from such changes in capacity, which we summarize in Figure~\ref{fig:sdid-financial}. Here, we see that converting hospitals' operating margins decline in the two years before designation, from about $-0.10$ to $-0.15$ in the state-timing sample, while the synthetic control declines only slightly. Converters' margins then recover after designation to a level closer to the control group than before the decline. We interpret the decline as the transitional cost of shedding scale before cost-based reimbursement begins and the subsequent recovery as the effect of that cost-plus reimbursement. The differential pre-trends in operating margins and expenses noted in Section~\ref{sec:financial} are consistent with the same restructuring.

While hospitals appear to begin restructuring in advance of designation, our main analysis maintains treatment as of the designation date. This is largely because cost-based reimbursement begins only at designation by construction. The restructuring that precedes designation is also only partial. A single treatment date taken at the time of designation therefore aligns with the official switch to cost-based reimbursement, and it keeps the estimates comparable across outcomes. Nonetheless, Table~\ref{tab:antic-state} reports the same state-timing estimates with treatment dated one year earlier, so that the year of most active restructuring is counted as treated. The estimated reduction in FTE RNs rises from $-2.0$ to $-3.2$ and in inpatient days per bed from $-6.7$ to $-9.7$, while the OB bed effect is unchanged and the total bed effect falls from $-4.1$ to $-2.6$, since the post-period average now includes a year of partial adjustment. The margin estimate roughly halves under the earlier dating (from 0.037 to 0.018 in the eligibility-restricted design), because the year of lowest margins is then counted as treated. The eligibility-restricted design shows the same pattern, with results in the supplemental appendix.

\subsection{Organizational Changes}
\label{sec:orgchange}

We next examine whether CAH designation altered hospitals’ propensity to exit, consolidate, or join a larger system. Section~\ref{sec:background} suggests two channels through which designation could reduce exit: 1) a higher average margin under cost-based reimbursement, and 2) a lower variance of Medicare revenue. Both mechanisms lower the chance that profits fall below some threshold at which a hospital closes.

Closures and mergers are relatively rare events at the hospital level, so we aggregate that analysis to state-year counts. This aggregation also accommodates any within-state spillovers across hospitals. Treatment timing is then defined as the first observed CAH designation in a state, with staggered state adoption between 1999 and 2002. To aid interpretation and comparability, we rescale SDID estimates and plotted paths by the mean pre-period hospital count in treated states, reporting results as counts per 100 hospitals. System membership, by contrast, is measured at the hospital-year level and estimated using the same hospital-level design as the capacity and financial outcomes.

Figure~\ref{fig:sdid-org-change} presents SDID results for (a) closures, (b) mergers, and (c) system membership. The synthetic controls track pre-designation trajectories reasonably well, although the pre-period series is noisier for closures and mergers because these events are relatively rare.

We find meaningful reductions in closures following CAH designation, where the SDID estimate implies a decline of 0.483 closures per 100 hospitals (95\% CI $[-0.83, -0.13]$).\footnote{Because the state-level design has few treated units, we also report permutation inference in Table~\ref{tab:sdid-overall}, which gives a $p$-value of 0.008 for closures. The supplemental appendix describes the procedure and reports the placebo distributions for all outcomes.}
By contrast, we find no evidence of a systematic effect on merger activity. In this case, the SDID estimate for mergers is 0.111 per 100 hospitals, with a confidence interval that includes zero (95\% CI $[-0.19, 0.41]$). Finally, at the hospital level, the SDID estimate shows a modest increase in system membership (0.074, 95\% CI $[0.01, 0.14]$), suggesting some movement toward affiliation even as closures decline. The CS estimates are generally comparable to the SDID estimates, albeit smaller in magnitude for closures.\footnote{The CS estimate for closures is $-0.278$ (95\% CI $[-0.65, 0.09]$), an interval that contains both zero and the SDID estimate. The difference appears to come from the later cohorts, where the not-yet-treated pool falls to 13 states in 2000 and five in 2001, each with cohort-specific CS estimates near zero. In the 1999 cohort, which has 21 control states, the CS estimate is larger in magnitude than the SDID estimate.}

This pattern of results is consistent with the incentive structure of CAH designation, as system affiliation may allow the acquiring entity to benefit from the hospital's cost-based reimbursement while a full merger or acquisition could jeopardize the hospital's CAH status. Taken together, the organizational-change results indicate that CAH designation primarily operates on the extensive margin of survival rather than affecting consolidation. In aggregate, our SDID estimate of $0.483$ fewer closures per 100 hospitals implies roughly 21 closures prevented across the approximately 4,400 hospitals in states that adopted the program. Allocated across the roughly 1,240 hospitals that had converted to CAH status by 2005, this translates to a per-converter reduction in closure probability of about 1.7\%, comparable to the 1.9\% implied by \cite{carroll2025}.


\subsection{Robustness: Alternative Control Group}
\label{sec:robustness_elig}

Our main identification strategy relies on the staggered timing of state-level CAH program adoption to construct control groups of not-yet-treated hospitals. While this design limits contamination from within-state selection, it restricts both the number of treated cohorts (1999--2001) and the control pool. To assess the sensitivity of our results to this particular control group construction, we implement an alternative eligibility-restricted design that relaxes the state-timing requirement entirely.

In the eligibility-restricted design, the treated group consists of hospitals that convert to CAH status in a given year, and the control group includes all non-CAH hospitals with 50 or fewer beds, both those that never convert and those that have not yet converted, regardless of whether their state has adopted the CAH program. This design widens the cohort range (1999--2005, yielding seven cohorts rather than three) and with it the treated sample, drawing on a control pool of 1,138 non-CAH small hospitals. The tradeoff is that the control group may include hospitals that chose not to convert despite eligibility, reintroducing potential selection concerns that the state-timing design was intended to mitigate. Nevertheless, the design provides a useful check on whether our main estimates are driven by the specific control group construction or by the state-timing restriction itself.

Figure~\ref{fig:forest-sdid} summarizes the comparison, plotting SDID ATT estimates and 95\% confidence intervals from the state-timing and eligibility-restricted designs side by side for each outcome. The capacity effects (total beds, OB beds, and FTE RNs) are robust across both designs and often larger in magnitude in the eligibility-restricted specification, consistent with later-adopting cohorts (2002--2005) experiencing similar or stronger downsizing. Financial results are also consistent across designs. The operating margin effect is 0.033 in the state-timing design and 0.037 in the eligibility-restricted design, and net patient revenue and operating expenses per bed decline in both, with expenses falling by more. System membership is consistent in sign, while the current ratio is small and insignificant under both designs. Full results from the eligibility-restricted design are reported in the supplemental appendix.


\subsection{Heterogeneity}
\label{sec:heterogeneity}

We examine heterogeneity in treatment effects along four dimensions: hospital ownership (government versus nonprofit), geographic isolation (distance to nearest hospital above versus below the treated-hospital median), pre-treatment financial health (operating margin above versus below the treated-hospital median, measured over 1996--1998), and pre-treatment system membership. For each dimension, we estimate SDID separately for each subgroup using the eligibility-restricted design, which provides greater statistical power for subgroup analysis with seven cohorts and a larger treated sample. Figure~\ref{fig:het-forest} summarizes the results. Because the figure involves 43 subgroup comparisons, we describe only the differences that remain statistically significant after a Bonferroni adjustment and treat the others as suggestive.

Capacity reductions following CAH designation are pervasive across all subgroups. Three differences remain significant after the adjustment, by ownership and by pre-treatment financial health. Private nonprofit hospitals reduce OB beds and operating expenses per bed by more than government hospitals ($-0.92$ versus $-0.39$ OB beds, and $-40$ versus $-8$ thousand dollars per bed), consistent with government hospitals facing additional constraints that limit downsizing. Hospitals with above-median pre-treatment margins also reduce OB beds by more than those below the median ($-0.94$ versus $-0.37$).

Finally, isolated and proximate hospitals both reduce beds and staffing at comparable magnitudes. Proximate hospitals show a significant increase in system membership post-CAH (ATT $= 0.076$, $p < 0.05$) while isolated hospitals do not, a difference that is not significant after the adjustment but is consistent with system membership being more feasible for hospitals with nearby potential partners, and with the broader trend of increasing CAH system affiliation documented in recent years \citep{whaley2024-jama}. The margin effect is positive and of similar size in every subgroup, between 0.025 and 0.046, consistent with the main results.



\section{Implications for Access and Health}
\label{sec:access_health}

Our estimates imply two offsetting margins of adjustment for inpatient capacity. CAH designation reduces hospital closures, preserving capacity that would otherwise disappear, but it also induces within-hospital resizing among converting facilities. These two effects involve largely different hospitals. Roughly 1,240 hospitals converted and shed capacity, and they were on average in better financial condition than the hospitals that closed over this period (see the supplemental appendix), while the closure effect corresponds to roughly 21 hospitals kept open. In this section, we quantify this tradeoff through an illustrative accounting exercise and then examine the health implications for patients displaced by capacity reductions at converting hospitals.

\subsection{Aggregate Capacity Accounting}
\label{sec:net_capacity}

Let $\Delta B$ denote the estimated change in total beds per converting (CAH) hospital, let $\Delta C$ denote the estimated change in closures per 100 hospitals, and let $\bar B_{\text{close}}$ denote the average number of beds among the marginal hospitals that would otherwise close absent CAH designation. If a share $\rho$ of hospitals convert to CAH status over the relevant post period, then the implied net change in total beds per hospital is
\begin{equation}
\Delta \text{Beds}^{net} =
\left(\frac{-\Delta C}{100}\right)\bar B_{\text{close}} +
\rho \cdot \Delta B,
\label{eq:net_beds}
\end{equation}
where the first term captures beds preserved per hospital via avoided closures and the second term captures beds lost (or gained) through within-hospital changes among CAH converters.

Our pooled SDID estimates give $\Delta C=-0.483$ closures per 100 hospitals and $\Delta B=-4.081$ beds per converting hospital. For the conversion share we set $\rho=0.30$, reflecting the approximately 1,240 hospitals that had converted to CAH status by 2005 as a share of the roughly 4,400 hospitals in states that adopted the program.\footnote{We define $\rho$ relative to all hospitals rather than a subset of ``eligible'' hospitals because the closure rate $\Delta C$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:net_beds} is also measured per 100 hospitals statewide. Among hospitals with 50 or fewer beds, the observed conversion rate is substantially higher (approximately 69\%), but using this rate would require rescaling $\Delta C$ to the same eligible denominator.} To proxy $\bar B_{\text{close}}$, we use the mean pre-CAH bed size from Table~\ref{tab:summary-by-cah}, $\bar B_{\text{close}}\approx 55$. Eq.~\eqref{eq:net_beds} then yields $\left(\frac{0.483}{100}\right)\times 55 + 0.30\times(-4.081) \approx 0.266 - 1.224 = -0.958$. Thus, under these assumptions, the point estimates imply a net reduction of approximately one bed per hospital. The calculation treats both effects as one-time changes in stocks that persist over the post-designation window, namely the closure effect as roughly 21 hospitals kept open, each with $\bar B_{\text{close}}$ beds, and the bed effect as a reduction of $\Delta B$ beds at each converting hospital.

We can further calculate the break-even conversion share at which preserved beds from avoided closures exactly offset resizing among converters as
\begin{equation}
\rho^{*}
=
\frac{\left(\frac{-\Delta C}{100}\right)\bar B_{\text{close}}}{-\Delta B}
\approx 0.065,
\label{eq:rho_star_beds}
\end{equation}
or about 7\% of hospitals. When $\rho$ exceeds this threshold, as in our setting with $\rho$ around 0.30, the implied net effect on total beds is negative. An analogous calculation using inpatient days yields a break-even of approximately 14\%, again well below the conversion rate of 30\% among all hospitals in adopting states. In other words, even if all CAH converters were already under-capacity at the time of conversion, so that bed counts could decrease without affecting patient care, the effects purely from reduced inpatient days per bed would still imply a negative net effect on availability of hospital care. Accounting for both margins simultaneously (fewer beds and lower utilization per remaining bed) would yield an even lower threshold. Our results therefore suggest that within-hospital downsizing more than offsets any capacity preserved through avoided closures, whether measured in beds or inpatient days.

The break-even share depends on the size of the marginal hospital saved from closure, since a smaller hospital kept open preserves fewer beds. Eq.~\eqref{eq:rho_star_beds} sets that size at the mean converter's 55 beds, but the small and rural hospitals that close are smaller than the average converter, so the baseline likely overstates the beds an averted closure preserves. Table~\ref{tab:breakeven-sens} recomputes the break-even share under alternatives. Hospitals with 50 or fewer beds that closed over 1995--2010 averaged 39 beds, which gives a break-even share of 4.6 percent, and rural closers averaged 47 beds, which gives 5.6 percent. If the hospitals saved from closure are the smallest ones, with 27 or 20 beds (the 25th and 10th percentiles among small hospitals), the break-even share falls to 3.2 and 2.4 percent. In every case, the net change in beds per hospital at the observed 30 percent conversion share is between $-0.96$ and $-1.13$. Under a wide range of reasonable assumptions, therefore, the reduced capacity among converters more than offsets the capacity saved through averted closures.

The point estimates underlying this accounting rely on several parameters estimated with uncertainty. We propagate this uncertainty through 10,000 Monte Carlo draws from the following distributions: $\Delta B \sim N(\hat\Delta_B, \hat\sigma_B^2)$ and $\Delta C \sim N(\hat\Delta_C, \hat\sigma_C^2)$ using the SDID point estimates and standard errors; $\rho \sim U(0.15, 0.45)$ spanning plausible overall conversion rates; and $\bar B_{\text{close}}$ drawn with replacement from the observed bed counts of the 79 hospitals with 50 or fewer beds that closed over 1995--2010 (mean 39). Figure~\ref{fig:psa-beds} displays the resulting distribution of net capacity effects. The median is approximately $-0.96$ beds per hospital, and only about 4\% of draws yield a positive net effect.\footnote{\cite{gowrisankaran2018} evaluate the program through a structural demand model and find that consumer welfare improves only if CAH designation prevents closure of at least 6.5\% of converting hospitals. Our break-even threshold of approximately 7\% addresses a different object (net bed capacity rather than consumer surplus), but both exercises point to the same tension wherein CAH participation constraints generate capacity costs that appear large relative to the closure-prevention benefits.}

The same estimates give a rough sense of how the subsidy is distributed. Cost-based reimbursement is paid to every hospital that converts, while the program's purpose, preserving access, is served through the hospitals it keeps open. Our data do not measure Medicare's outlays directly, since the cost report's Medicare payment field applies to prospective payment rather than to hospitals paid on a cost basis, but the margin effect gives the gain to converting hospitals. A margin gain of 3.3 to 3.7 percentage points on mean pre-designation net patient revenue of about \$11 million (in 2010 dollars) is roughly \$0.4 million of added net income per converting hospital per year, or about \$0.5 billion per year across the roughly 1,240 converters. Set against the roughly 21 closures averted, that is on the order of \$22 to \$24 million of added net income per year for each hospital kept open. This is a program-level accounting that does not value the reduction in inpatient scale, but it shows that the program is an expensive way to prevent closures because it is untargeted. Most of the transfer goes to hospitals at relatively low risk of closure, and those hospitals reduce inpatient scale to receive it.

\subsection{Patient Health}
\label{sec:patient_health}

The aggregate capacity accounting quantifies net reductions in beds or inpatient days but does not address the health consequences for patients affected by hospital downsizing. We consider these consequences through two channels: closures prevented and capacity reduced at surviving hospitals.

For the closure-prevention channel, we draw on \cite{gujral2019}, who find that rural hospital closures increase inpatient mortality for time-sensitive conditions by 0.78 percentage points. We do not estimate this effect ourselves, but taking it as given offers a sense of the health benefit implied by our closure estimate. With roughly 21 closures prevented across CAH states and approximately 1,000 admissions per year at each, the implied benefit is about 170 lives saved annually, or 50 to 290 lives based on our 95\% confidence interval of 6 to 36 closures averted. We stress, however, that these figures are illustrative, since they apply a mortality effect estimated in a different setting to our closure estimate.

The health effects of the capacity-reduction channel are less clear. Our SDID point estimates indicate approximately 6.7 fewer inpatient days per bed at converting hospitals, a meaningful reduction in care delivered at these facilities, albeit imprecisely estimated. For each CAH converter, the next-nearest hospital is a median of approximately 18 miles away, with an interquartile range of 12 to 24 miles. This is much farther than the overall hospital median of 12 miles and reflects the rural geography and general isolation of CAH-eligible communities. Figure~\ref{fig:map-cah-exposure} illustrates the geographic concentration of these effects, shading counties whose nearest hospital is a CAH converter by the additional travel distance to the next non-CAH alternative. The most exposed counties are concentrated in the Great Plains, Mountain West, and upper Midwest, where the next alternative may be 30 miles or more away.

Whether patients displaced by capacity reductions forgo care or travel to more distant facilities is beyond the scope of our data. That said, outpatient volume at converting hospitals is largely unchanged and emergency visits fall by roughly 17 percent (Section~\ref{sec:capacity}), so the displaced inpatient care does not appear to be absorbed into outpatient care at the same hospital. The combination of reduced inpatient capacity and geographic isolation therefore suggests that any welfare costs from downsizing may be concentrated in precisely the communities the CAH program was designed to protect.

Evidence from \cite{gowrisankaran2018} provides insight into which patients are most affected by conversion. Using patient-level discharge data from seven states, they find that CAH conversion reduces admissions by approximately 5\%, with the largest reductions among high-acuity conditions (AMI admissions decline by 8\% and injury-related admissions by 12\%), while lower-acuity conditions such as pneumonia and respiratory disease are relatively unaffected. The health effects of this redirection are theoretically ambiguous. Patients with time-sensitive conditions face longer travel to reach care, which may worsen outcomes. At the same time, these patients arrive at larger, better-equipped hospitals capable of more complex interventions. Even within a single service line, the evidence points in both directions. For example, \cite{kozhimannil2018} find that loss of rural obstetric services increases preterm births by 0.67 percentage points, while \cite{fischer2024} find that OB unit closures have negligible or slightly beneficial effects as women receive higher-quality care at centralized facilities. To our knowledge, there is limited causal evidence that directly estimates the health effects of capacity reductions among incumbent hospitals, in contrast to the well-studied case of complete hospital closure. CAH conversion, which legislates capacity contraction without closure, provides a natural setting for studying this question, but doing so requires patient-level discharge data linked to conversion timing, which we leave to future work.


\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:conclusions}

This paper examines the effects of Critical Access Hospital designation on hospital finances, capacity, staffing, and organizational outcomes. Our central finding is that CAH designation achieves its primary goal of reducing hospital closures, but it does so at the cost of operational scale. Converting hospitals shed inpatient beds, consistent with the program's eligibility requirements, along with declines in obstetric capacity, nursing staff, and emergency volume. Operating margins improve, because cost-based reimbursement sustains revenue while expenses fall with scale. The result is a ``survival versus scale'' tradeoff across hospitals within a state, in which designation averts closure for a small number of hospitals while the many more hospitals that convert continue operating at a smaller inpatient scale.

The capacity effects are the most precisely estimated results in our analysis. They are robust across different control group constructions, different estimators including interactive fixed effects models, and alternative methods of inference such as permutation inference. The results are also stable across alternative bed size cutoffs for sample eligibility and across varying lags in the state-level adoption timing used to define control groups. All of these robustness exercises are reported in the supplemental appendix. The heterogeneity analysis further shows that these reductions are pervasive across hospital ownership types, levels of geographic isolation, and pre-treatment financial health, reinforcing the interpretation that the effects reflect the program's design rather than selection by a particular subset of hospitals. Where capacity outcomes exhibit differential pre-trends at $t = -1$, this reflects the incentives of the CAH program itself, since hospitals must restructure operations to meet eligibility requirements before formal designation. Dating treatment one year earlier leaves the capacity effects similar, larger for staffing and inpatient days and smaller for beds, as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:anticipation}.

Our identification period coincides with several concurrent Medicare payment reforms, including the Balanced Budget Refinement Act of 1999 and the Benefits Improvement and Protection Act of 2000, which affected hospital reimbursement more broadly. While our design compares hospitals across states within narrow event windows, we cannot fully separate the effects of CAH designation from differential exposure to these concurrent changes, although we stress that such policies were intended to affect a much broader set of hospitals and therefore likely do not contribute meaningfully to any differential patterns between CAHs and non-CAHs over time.

The form of the eligibility rule also matters. The bed cap is the binding constraint in our results, and the effects on obstetric capacity and staffing follow from it. An eligibility rule based on inpatient volume or occupancy rather than staffed beds would remove the incentive to shed beds that would otherwise be used, at the cost of some of the program's control over inpatient scale. Our estimates do not address such alternatives, and we present them as possibilities for policy design rather than as conclusions from our analysis.

The distinction between preserving hospital presence and preserving pre-designation capacity has direct policy implications. As the capacity accounting in Section~\ref{sec:access_health} shows, the break-even conversion rate is approximately 7\% for beds and 14\% for inpatient days, well below observed conversion rates in states that adopted the program, and the resulting capacity reductions are concentrated in precisely the rural areas the program was designed to serve. The program is also an expensive way to prevent closures, since most of the subsidy goes to hospitals at relatively low risk of closure (Section~\ref{sec:net_capacity}). The Rural Emergency Hospital (REH) designation, introduced in 2023 \citep{cms2022}, represents a further point along the survival-scale tradeoff, as REH status eliminates inpatient services entirely in exchange for enhanced outpatient reimbursement. Our results for CAH, where the capacity constraints are less severe, suggest that even modest eligibility restrictions can generate meaningful reductions in service scope. Closure prevention confers clear mortality benefits, but the health effects of capacity reductions at surviving hospitals remain an open empirical question. Resolving this question requires patient-level discharge data linked to conversion timing, an important direction for future work given that the CAH program provides one of the cleanest natural experiments in legislated capacity contraction.
